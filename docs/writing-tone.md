# Writing Tone — Brandon Olin

Reference this when drafting blog posts. The goal is that a reader who knows Brandon can't tell the difference.

---

## Voice in one sentence

Curious, direct, self-aware. Thinks out loud. Doesn't pretend to have more figured out than he does.

---

## How posts open

Drop straight into a scene, a strong opinion, or a provocation. No throat-clearing, no "In this post I will cover."

**Good:**
> "Day 1 of the Europe trip I've been looking forward to for months, and I'm stuck at an airport hotel in Philadelphia."

> "The world is hell bent on keeping you driving."

**Not this:**
> "Today I want to talk about an interesting problem I encountered while building Hearth..."

The opening should make you want to keep reading, not orient you to what's coming.

---

## Sentence rhythm

Mix of short punchy standalone lines and longer flowing ones. When making a point with weight, let it breathe on its own line. Stacking short sentences is a deliberate tool:

> Rise and grind.
> I'll sleep when I'm dead.
> No rest for the weary.

Don't be afraid of a one-sentence paragraph.

---

## Swearing

Natural and unpretentious. "bitching and moaning about airline fuckery", "3 fuckin times", "fucking exhausting." Not every paragraph, not for shock value — just when it fits the thought.

---

## Profanity-adjacent: the self-deprecating aside

Brandon undercuts his own gravity regularly. He'll make a point, then puncture it slightly:

> "Post-trip update with a 1.5 month delay to ponder further: I didn't actually wait this long intentionally to give myself time to process the ordeal, I'm just lazy and forgetful."

> "Ok, that's enough trite wisdom for a weeks worth of wannabe Instagram life coach shitposting."

This is a core part of the voice. Don't skip it.

---

## Parenthetical asides

Used like a quiet whispered comment. "(a completed version would probably help me there!)" — keeps the narrative flow without breaking into a new sentence.

---

## Italics and emphasis

Used selectively for key concepts or to give a word the stress it would carry in speech. Not for decoration.

> "let's talk about the *what* and *why*"
> "*give myself permission to rest*"

---

## Section headers (H2s)

Conversational and a bit playful, not formal. They read like chapter titles, not outline bullets.

> "When god closes a door…"
> "Is it always sunny in Philadelphia?"
> "Wherever you go, there you are"

For tech posts, headers can be more direct but should still have personality.

---

## References and citations

Quotes Seneca, Wikipedia definitions, Derek Sivers, Jordan Peterson, pop psychology terms — casually, without being academic about it. He explains why a reference is relevant, he doesn't just drop it in.

---

## Pop culture

Used naturally when it fits. Iron Man/Jarvis as an analogy for a life assistant. It's Always Sunny as a frame for optimism. Michael Franti as a closer. Never forced.

---

## Self-doubt and counterpoint

Brandon often presents his own counterargument, especially on softer topics:

> "I sincerely hope it's the former, but the residual echoes in my mind from the culture I grew up in are worried it's the latter."

This is what makes the writing feel honest rather than advice-column-y. Don't always resolve the tension — it's okay to leave a question open.

---

## Tech writing specifically

The voice doesn't change when writing about code. He writes about technical topics the way he'd explain them to a smart friend who isn't necessarily a developer. He:

- Uses analogies (Jarvis, rocket boosters on output)
- Acknowledges his own limits ("it would take me a painfully long time to write those SQL queries by hand")
- Gets genuinely excited without being hype-y
- Ends with honest uncertainty rather than a triumphant conclusion

**Don't switch to "developer blog" mode.** No "Let's dive in." No "As you can see from the code snippet above." Keep it human.

---

## Bullet points and lists

Used sparingly and only when a list is actually the right format — tech stack details, structured phases, concrete examples. Not for philosophical points or emotional content.

---

## The closing

Short, personal, no grand wrap-up paragraph. Sometimes just a piece of media and a sign-off. He signs his name.

> "Till then, Brandon"
> "C'est la vie! 🤘"
> "I'll keep you updated as I progress, til next time! Brandon"

Don't write a summary conclusion. When the thought is done, stop.

---

## Things to avoid

- "In this post, I will cover..." — never
- "In conclusion..." — never
- Explaining the structure of the post before writing it
- Over-using bullet points to organize thoughts that should be prose
- Sounding like a LinkedIn post — no "excited to share", no "the learnings were incredible"
- Resolving every tension neatly — real posts leave some things open
- Switching to formal/technical voice for code topics — the voice stays the same

---

## The meta rule

If it sounds like a blog post written by an AI trying to sound like a person, rewrite it. It should sound like a person who happens to use complete sentences.
